00 · History

How the System Got Here

The process and authorities below weren't designed on a blank page — they accumulated over 150+ years of trade, treaty law, disaster, and negotiation. A short history of how Puget Sound went from canoe routes to the fourth-largest container gateway in North America.

Time immemorial

Coast Salish maritime routes

Coast Salish peoples navigated the Sound for millennia in cedar canoes — six distinct types built for war, freight, fishing, and travel — maintaining trade networks reaching as far as the Aleutians. Early settlers depended on Tribal paddlers and guides to get around at all.

Source: Job Carr Museum, Maritime Washington

1868

Washington Territory regulates pilotage

Territorial law establishes a three-member pilotage commission — the legal root of the compulsory pilotage system Puget Sound Pilots still operates under today.

Source: Puget Sound Pilots

c. 1860s–1930s

The Mosquito Fleet

A loosely organized armada of privately run steamboats — estimated as large as 2,500 vessels — connects every shoreline town with a dock. It's the primary way people, mail, livestock, and freight move around the Sound, until automobiles and highways make it obsolete by the early 1930s.

Source: HistoryLink.org

1900

Puget Sound Navigation Co. (the Black Ball Line)

Walter Oakes and Charles Peabody incorporate PSN, which pioneers auto-ferry service across the Sound and comes to dominate cross-Sound travel for the next five decades.

Source: HistoryLink.org

1911

Port of Seattle created

King County voters approve a public port district 13,771 to 4,538, breaking the private railroads' grip on Seattle's waterfront under the newly passed Port District Act.

Source: HistoryLink.org

1918

Port of Tacoma created

Pierce County voters approve their own public port district, setting up seven decades of head-to-head competition with Seattle for cargo.

Source: Port of Tacoma

1935

Washington State Pilotage Act

The state formalizes a five-member Board of Pilotage Commissioners and the Puget Sound Pilots organization, requiring foreign vessels and U.S. ships in foreign trade to carry a state-licensed pilot.

Source: Puget Sound Pilots

1951

Washington State Ferries begins operating

After the state buys out PSN's ships and routes for $5 million in 1949, WSF starts service on June 1, 1951 — today the largest ferry system in the U.S. by ridership.

Source: HistoryLink.org

1964

First modern container ship out of Puget Sound

Matson's Hawaiian Builder sails from the Port of Seattle's Pier 46, two years after the Port committed $30 million to container infrastructure before any shipping line had signed on — a bet that put Seattle ahead of competing West Coast ports.

Source: HistoryLink.org

1972

Vessel Traffic Service Puget Sound established

Created under the new federal Ports and Waterways Safety Act, giving the Coast Guard a formal, 24/7 traffic-management system for the Strait of Juan de Fuca, San Juan Islands, and Puget Sound. Since 1979 it's run cooperatively with the Canadian Coast Guard.

Source: USCG Navigation Center

2015

Northwest Seaport Alliance formed

Port of Seattle and Port of Tacoma sign an unprecedented agreement to jointly operate their container terminals as one gateway — now the fourth-largest in North America, moving roughly 3.7 million containers a year.

Source: HistoryLink.org

01 · Process

Vessel & Cargo Process Flow

A commercial vessel calling on Puget Sound follows a fixed sequence of regulatory checkpoints and handoffs. Alongside it runs a parallel technology flow — the bridge, engine, and terminal systems that make each stage possible, and the surface where operational technology (OT) security matters most.

Commercial & Regulatory Flow
Step 1

Arrival

Vessel submits a Notice of Arrival to USCG/CBP up to 96 hours out. Vessel Traffic Service (VTS) Puget Sound tracks the ship inbound through the Strait of Juan de Fuca.

→
Step 2

Pilotage

A state-commissioned Puget Sound Pilot boards near Port Angeles and conns the vessel through restricted, high-traffic waters — compulsory for most foreign and non-enrolled vessels.

→
Step 3

Berth / Terminal Assignment

The receiving terminal (Port of Seattle or an NWSA-managed Tacoma terminal) confirms a berth window and lines up tugs, linehandlers, and crane scheduling.

→
Step 4

Customs & Border Protection

CBP conducts arrival inspection and manifest/crew review; USCG Sector Puget Sound verifies MTSA/ISPS security compliance for the vessel and facility.

→
Step 5

Cargo & Logistics Handling

ILWU longshore labor and terminal crews work the vessel under the terminal operating system; customs brokers clear cargo, and rail (BNSF/UP) or drayage trucking moves it inland.

→
Step 6

Departure

The pilot re-boards for outbound transit, VTS sequences the vessel into open traffic, and USCG monitors until it clears the pilotage grounds.

Parallel OT / ICS Flow
Layer A

Bridge & ECDIS

ECDIS, radar/ARPA, AIS, and GNSS positioning — the navigation OT layer the pilot and deck officers work through during pilotage and transit.

→
Layer B

Engine Control Systems

Propulsion control, ballast management, and alarm-monitoring systems in the engine room — the vessel's core industrial control surface.

→
Layer C

Port Terminal OT

Terminal operating system, ship-to-shore crane controls, and gate/OCR systems — where the vessel's IT/OT boundary extends onto shore infrastructure.

This OT layer runs underneath every stage on the left — it's the operational technology surface covered in depth on the companion Maritime OT (MOT-101) site.

02 · Structure

Authority Structure

No single body runs Puget Sound. Federal and state regulators set the rules, a private pilot association bridges regulation and vessel operations, and a mix of municipal, joint, and state bodies actually run the ports and ferries — each with a different mandate.

Federal & State Regulatory Authority

Federal

USCG Sector Puget Sound

Safety, security, and environmental-response authority for all Puget Sound waterways. Runs VTS Puget Sound and enforces MTSA/ISPS compliance across ports, vessels, and pilots alike.

State

WA Board of Pilotage Commissioners

State body that licenses, rate-sets, and disciplines Puget Sound Pilots. Regulatory authority over the pilotage profession specifically, not over ports or terminals.

|

Operational Bridge

Operational

Puget Sound Pilots

Private association of state-commissioned pilots. Licensed and disciplined by the state, but embedded in vessel operations — the working bridge between regulation and the ship itself.

|

Commercial & Public Operating Authorities

Municipal

Port of Seattle

Municipal port district. Retains cruise, general cargo, real estate, and Sea-Tac Airport operations directly; contributes container terminals to NWSA.

Municipal

Port of Tacoma

Municipal port district. Retains bulk, breakbulk, and log terminals directly; contributes container terminals to NWSA.

Joint venture

Northwest Seaport Alliance (NWSA)

Marine cargo operating partnership formed in 2015, jointly governed by the Seattle and Tacoma port commissions. Manages container terminal operations for both ports as one gateway.

State agency

Washington State Ferries (WSF)

A WSDOT division, not a port authority. Operates the largest ferry system in the U.S. by ridership, sharing the same waterways and VTS but running outside the deep-sea cargo and pilotage system.

Regulatory (sets/enforces rules) Operational bridge (licensed, but hands-on) Commercial / public operator (runs the infrastructure)

03 · Careers

Career Map

Each role sits at a specific point in the process flow from Section 01. But nobody starts at the top — each one below is a ladder: the entry point, the certifications and experience that unlock the next rung, and where that climb tops out. Sourced against actual USCG, Puget Sound Pilots, CBP, and GIAC requirements.

⚓

Deck Officers (Master & Mates)

Command and navigate the vessel, work directly with the boarding pilot, and hold ultimate responsibility for the ship through arrival, pilotage, and departure.

ArrivalPilotageDepartureBridge/ECDIS
Rung 1
Third Mate

Entry point after a maritime academy or USCG-approved deck program, STCW Basic Safety Training, and a TWIC card. First licensed sea time starts accumulating here as Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch (OICNW).

Requires: deck license exam + entry sea time
→
Rung 2
Second Mate

Builds further OICNW sea time and adds cargo/stability and celestial navigation exam modules to the license.

Requires: additional OICNW sea time
→
Rung 3
Chief Mate

OICNW sea time counts 2-for-1 toward this tier. Adds management-level modules: ship stability, medical care, and crew leadership.

Requires: 360 days OICNW-equivalent sea time
→
Rung 4
Master

Needs 360 days as Chief Mate (at least 180 of them specifically as Chief Mate), or 720 days as Master on Great Lakes/Inland waters. Must log 90 days' sea time every 7 years to keep the license current.

Ceiling role — feeds into Marine Pilots below

Engine Officers (Chief Engineer & Engineers)

Operate and maintain propulsion, power generation, and ballast systems — the engine room side of the vessel that keeps it maneuverable through every stage.

Engine Control Systems
Rung 1
QMED

Qualified Member of the Engineering Department — the entry-level engine room rating and the sea-time foundation every engineering license is built on.

Requires: entry-level engine room rating
→
Rung 2
Third Assistant Engineer

720 days as QMED (or 1,080 days as a machinist apprentice with 360 in the engine room, or a maritime academy degree plus 90 days), then a USCG license exam.

Requires: 720 days QMED sea time
→
Rung 3
Second Assistant Engineer

At least one year of experience as Third Assistant Engineer.

Requires: 1+ year as Third A/E
→
Rung 4
First Assistant Engineer

Over one year of experience as Second Assistant Engineer.

Requires: 1+ year as Second A/E
→
Rung 5
Chief Engineer

Top of the engineering department — full responsibility for propulsion, power generation, and ballast systems fleet-wide.

Ceiling role
🔒

Marine Pilots

State-commissioned specialists in local waters who board inbound and outbound vessels to conn them through Puget Sound's restricted channels — recruited from finished ship-handlers, not trained from scratch.

Pilotage
Rung 1
Master Mariner (prerequisite)

Must already hold an unlimited Master's license and have sailed as Master for 2–4 years before even applying. Age 25–70 to be licensed.

Requires: unlimited Master's license + 2–4 yrs command
→
Rung 2
Applicant

Applies through the WA Board of Pilotage Commissioners' open exam cycle, then sits a written exam and a simulator evaluation.

Requires: passing written exam + simulator eval
→
Rung 3
Ranked & Called

Scoring above the cut places a candidate on a ranked list; candidates are called into the training program for their district as positions open.

Requires: rank placement, open position
→
Rung 4
Trainee Pilot

Supervised training period conning vessels through Puget Sound's restricted waters under a licensed pilot before being released to work solo.

Requires: supervised transits
→
Rung 5
Licensed Pilot

Full state-commissioned pilot, regulated and disciplined by the WA Board of Pilotage Commissioners for the rest of the career.

Ceiling role
🏦

Port Operations

Terminal superintendents, marine operations staff, and equipment operators at Port of Seattle, Port of Tacoma, and NWSA who assign berths, schedule cranes, and coordinate tugs and linehandling.

Berth AssignmentCargo Handling
Rung 1
Casual Longshore Worker

Entered through the ILWU hiring hall; work is assigned shift by shift while hours accumulate toward union membership.

Requires: hiring-hall registration
→
Rung 2
Registered Longshore Worker

After roughly 2–3 years of accumulated hours and safety training, gains full ILWU registration and specializes in crane, yard equipment, or lashing.

Requires: ~2–3 yrs hours + safety training
→
Rung 3
Foreman / Terminal Operator

Steady, non-casual role directing a gang on a shift and operating specialized terminal equipment.

Requires: steady registration status
→
Rung 4
Terminal Superintendent

Directly supervises ILWU foremen and hall labor; manages container yards, marine operations, gang hiring, and discipline for the whole terminal.

Ceiling role
🔐

OT / ICS Security Engineers

Secure the industrial control systems underneath bridge, engine, and terminal operations — a cross-cutting role that spans the entire OT flow rather than one stage.

Bridge/ECDISEngine Control SystemsPort Terminal OT
Rung 1
IT or Controls Foundation

Comes in from enterprise IT (networking, systems administration) or industrial controls (automation/instrumentation) — needs working fluency in both to be useful in OT.

Requires: IT or automation background
→
Rung 2
GICSP

The GIAC Global Industrial Cyber Security Professional cert is the field's benchmark: validates the ability to secure Purdue Model Levels 0–3 without disrupting the physical process.

Requires: GICSP certification
→
Rung 3
Specialization Track

Technical: deepen into hands-on ICS security engineering. Compliance: pursue the ISA/IEC 62443 series (Fundamentals → Risk Assessment → Design & Maintenance). Leadership: add CISSP as the strategic layer.

Requires: chosen specialization cert
→
Rung 4
Maritime OT Specialist

Applies that foundation to vessels and terminals specifically — IACS UR E26/E27 cyber resilience requirements, ECDIS/engine hardening, terminal IT/OT boundaries. Deep-dive on the companion Maritime OT site.

Ceiling role
📋

Logistics & Customs Brokers

Clear cargo through CBP, manage manifests and duties, and coordinate the intermodal handoff to rail and drayage trucking once containers leave the terminal.

Customs & CBPCargo Handling
Rung 1
Brokerage Trainee

No degree required — learns entry, classification, and documentation on the job as an import/export coordinator.

Requires: no formal degree
→
Rung 2
Exam Candidate

Studies 3–6 months for the Customs Broker License Exam: a 4.5-hour, 80-question, open-book test covering entry, valuation, classification, trade agreements, and compliance. Pass rates run 15–25% per sitting.

Requires: 75%+ on the CBLE
→
Rung 3
Licensed Customs Broker

Files a license application via the eCBP portal within 3 years of passing, pays the fees, and secures CBP approval.

Requires: CBP license approval
→
Rung 4
Practicing Broker

Clears cargo through CBP, manages bonds/duties/valuation, and coordinates the intermodal handoff once containers leave the terminal.

Ceiling role
